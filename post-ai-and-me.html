AI and Me: What 1 Year in Tech Taught Me

AI is changing software development rapidly — but not in the way most people think. Why code is only the visible layer of engineering, and why fundamentals matter now more than ever.

"Tools change. Fundamentals don't. Keep building." — Sabarnik Lahiri

After spending the last year interning and working as a Software Engineer, one thing has become very clear to me:

AI is changing software development rapidly — but not in the way most people think.

The conversation online often swings between two extremes:

  • “AI will replace engineers entirely.”
  • “AI is just another autocomplete tool.”

From what I’ve experienced firsthand while building enterprise software, the reality sits squarely in between. AI dramatically increases speed. But engineering was never just about speed.

The Practical Superpower: Compressing Distance

Over the past year, I’ve routinely used AI for:

  • Understanding unfamiliar codebases: Quick summaries of architectural flows across large repositories.
  • Exploring frameworks faster: Synthesizing documentation into tailored idiomatic snippets.
  • Debugging edge cases: Spotting subtle off-by-one errors or concurrency quirks in async logic.
  • Generating repetitive boilerplate: Writing DTO mappings, test fixtures, and schema definitions.
  • Organizing my thoughts: Rubber-ducking solutions when I was stuck on complex business logic.

In many ways, it compresses the distance between not knowing and starting.

And that matters tremendously. Because one of the hardest parts of being an early-career engineer is not the complexity itself — it’s the intimidation that comes before you even begin. AI lowers that barrier and gives you the confidence to take the first step.

“AI can generate implementation. It cannot inherit ownership. And ownership is ultimately what engineering demands.”

Code Is Only the Visible Layer

The more I worked on production systems with real business impact, the more I realized something foundational:

Code is only the visible layer of engineering.

Underneath the code sits the true substance of software engineering:

  • Product decisions and business trade-offs
  • Scalability and failure modes
  • Imperfect legacy systems and technical debt
  • Team communication and cross-functional alignment
  • Years of accumulated business domain context that no model fully grasps

An LLM can generate 200 lines of syntactically valid code in seconds. But when that code causes a production race condition at 2:00 AM, the model isn't on call. You are. The engineer owns the outcome.

From Recall to Judgment

One of the more interesting side effects of AI is how it changes learning itself.

In previous generations, developers spent years memorizing syntax, patterns, and documentation structures because access to information was the bottleneck.

Today, information is immediate. The challenge is no longer: “How do I find an answer?”

It is: “How do I evaluate whether this answer deserves trust?”

That shift feels profound to me. Because it means the value of an engineer increasingly comes from judgment rather than recall.

What AI Can't Replace

The ability to ask precise questions, identify flawed assumptions, reason through ambiguity, and understand consequences beyond the code itself — these are human cognitive traits that cannot be automated.

The Dangerous Illusion of Competence

I’ve also realized that AI can create a dangerous illusion of competence:

  • Generated code often looks correct at first glance.
  • AI explanations sound immensely confident and convincing.
  • Architecture suggestions appear polished and enterprise-ready.

But software engineering has a relentless way of exposing shallow understanding very quickly — especially in production environments under high load.

Eventually, fundamentals catch up with everyone.

And maybe that’s why I’ve started appreciating fundamentals more, not less:

  • Data structures & algorithms: Knowing why a hash map or B-tree behaves the way it does.
  • System design: Understanding bottlenecks, partitioning, and consistency boundaries.
  • Concurrency: Reasoning about threads, race conditions, and deadlocks.
  • Clean abstractions: Building code that teammates can maintain two years from now.
  • Communication: Explaining technical decisions clearly to non-engineers.

AI accelerates execution, but fundamentals still determine direction.

The Road Ahead

After a year in tech, my perspective is simple: AI will absolutely reshape this industry. But I don’t think the most valuable engineers will be the ones who rely on AI the most.

I think they’ll be the ones who combine:

  1. Deep thinking
  2. Adaptability
  3. Technical fundamentals
  4. The wisdom to use AI without outsourcing their understanding

Still learning. Still making mistakes. Still discovering how much there is left to understand.

And honestly, that’s probably the best part of engineering.
